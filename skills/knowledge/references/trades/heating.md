# Trade: Underfloor heating (wet system) in the bathroom

Graph tasks: `underfloor-heating` (and the heating-up protocol in `screed`). Typical values: the system maker's installation manual wins.

**DIY level:** laying pipes and pressure testing is DIY-capable. **Connecting to the heat source/manifold, balancing, and setting temperatures** is best done by the heating installer. In many countries the heat-pump or boiler side is regulated.

## Wet (water) vs. electric mat: which is better?
| | Wet (water) UFH | Electric mat/cable |
|---|---|---|
| Build-up height | ≥ 45 mm screed + pipe = high | 3–10 mm in the tile adhesive |
| Running cost | low with a heat pump | higher (electricity) |
| Response | slow | fast |
| Needs | manifold, supply, thermostat | circuit + RCD + thermostat |
| Best when | new screed, whole-home UFH already | retrofit with little height, small rooms |
**Recommendation:** if the home already has a wet UFH manifold or heat pump: add a wet loop. For a small bathroom with limited height: an electric mat in a thin bed or a decoupling-mat system is often simpler. It also works with the room's radiator/towel rail.

## Materials
- Insulation panels with a studded or smooth surface (EPS ≥ 150 kPa compressive class for the load), thickness per the heat-loss rules of your country
- Pipe 16 × 2 mm (PE-RT or PEX with an oxygen barrier), clips or studs
- Edge strip, PE sheet
- Manifold with flow meters and valves, loop cabinet, thermostat and floor temperature limiter
- Conduit for the thermostat sensor

## Design rules (typical)
- **Pipe spacing:** 10 cm in bathrooms (10–15 cm elsewhere). Closer at the outer walls and windows (edge zones).
- **Loop length:** ≤ 100 m for 16 mm pipe (shorter for good balance). One loop for a small bathroom is common.
- **No pipes** below the WC frame anchor, the shower drain, **or under fixed furniture** unless the layout allows. Keep ≥ 10–15 cm clear of drain bodies, anchors and heavy fixings.
- **Maximum floor surface temperature** in bathrooms ≈ 33 °C (29 °C in living rooms).
- Flow temperature: low (35–40 °C) for a heat pump.
- Cover: ≥ 45 mm screed over the pipe (see screed).

## Method
1. **Plan the layout on paper** with the drain, the WC frame and doors. Photograph the plan.
2. Lay insulation tightly with taped joints, PE sheet over, edge strip around.
3. Lay the pipe loops with the same spacing and radii (min bend radius per pipe, typically ≥ 5× the diameter). Use clips or the studs at ≈ 50–70 cm.
4. **Pipe connections only at the manifold** (no joints in the screed).
5. Cross a movement joint only through a protective sleeve.
6. **Photograph** the layout with a tape measure in each corner. Keep the photo in the project file.
7. **Pressure test** (typical ≈ 6 bar for ≥ 24 h with no drop, or the maker's value) and **keep the pressure on while the screed is poured** (≈ 4 bar, say).
8. Thermostat sensor conduit with a sleeve end at the loop, **between two pipes**.

## Heating-up protocol
See `screed.md`: the screed is functional-heated no earlier than the day-21 mark (cement) and signed off. Don't start drying heating or tiling heat-ups before the right dates.

## After tiling
- Heating stays off or low until adhesive and grout have cured (typically ≥ 7 days), then increase **gradually**.
- Adjust the thermostat to the floor limit.

## Common mistakes
- Pipes too close to the shower drain or WC anchors: a drill or anchor hits them.
- Running the heating at full power without the protocol.
- No photographs of the pipe layout (needed later for CM sampling and drilling).
- Sensor placed above a pipe: false readings.
- Missing edge strip: the screed cracks as it heats.

## Radiators and towel rails (install)
**When:** the pipes are in during the rough-in and tested. **The radiator is hung after the wall is plastered and painted** (and after the wall is tiled in a bathroom).
1. **Position:** typically **under a window** or on the coldest wall, the **bottom ≈ 10–15 cm above the finished floor**, **≈ 3–5 cm from the wall**, with space for the valves. **Check the output** (W at the design flow temperature) against the room's heat loss.
2. **Backing and fixing:** brackets in **solid wall with the right anchors** (four for larger radiators), in **drywall onto backing**. **A filled radiator is heavy** (≈ 30–60 kg).
3. **Valves:** a **thermostatic valve** on the flow, a **lockshield (balancing) valve** on the return, **isolation** so the radiator can be removed. A **bleed valve** at the top.
4. **Connect** with the correct fittings (compression or push-fit as the pipe system), **no strain on the pipes**.
5. **Fill and bleed** the system, check the **pressure (≈ 1.5–2 bar cold)**, **balance** the lockshield valve per the installer.
6. **Paint** only with radiator paint (heat-resistant). **Don't cover** radiators with long curtains or furniture.
7. **Bathroom towel rail (water):** the same, with connections at the **bottom** or as the product sheet shows. **Electric towel rails** are an **electrical** installation: IP rating suitable for the zone (typically **IPX4** minimum), a **dedicated circuit with RCD, by the electrician**, a **thermostat or timer**.

## Whole house: heat source, radiators and zones
Graph task: `heating-distribution`. The heat source (boiler, heat pump, district heating) is a **licensed installer's** work in most countries. Planning and the in-room distribution are yours.
- **Heat loss per room:** calculate it (or have an installer estimate) **after** the planned insulation and window changes. A rough check: typical retrofit homes need ≈ 40–80 W per m² of floor area. A new window or an insulation layer can halve that.
- **Flow temperature drives the choice of emitters.** A **heat pump** is efficient at low flow temperatures (≈ 30–40 °C), so use **UFH or large low-temperature radiators**. A gas or oil boiler works with smaller radiators at ≈ 55–70 °C.
- **Radiators:** size per room (output at the design flow temperature). Panel radiators under windows, **thermostatic valves in each room**, space for air flow, hydraulic balancing, and **pipe routes planned** through walls or floors with isolation.
- **UFH vs. radiators per room:** UFH for bathrooms, kitchens and hallways with tiles. Radiators or UFH for living rooms. Radiators with a quick response for bedrooms. Don't cover UFH with thick carpets or heavy built-ins.
- **Zones and controls:** a room thermostat per zone, a weather-compensated control, a **night setback**, and a hot water schedule. Sensors in the right places (not near a stove).
- **Fill, flush and test:** flush the old system before adding a heat pump or a new boiler, add inhibitor, protect against corrosion and **magnetite**, **pressure test** the whole system and **balance** it.
- **Wood stove / fireplace:** the flue, the clearances and the combustion air supply are regulated. The chimney sweeper's check is required in many countries. Not DIY for the flue.
